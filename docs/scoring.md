# Scoring System

## Overview

Every company is scored on **8 KPIs**, each rated 0–3 (max total: 24). A company must meet both a **total score** threshold AND a **minimum passes** threshold to qualify.

### Why Two Thresholds?

A company that scores 3/3 on three KPIs but 0/3 on the other five has a total of 9 — which would classify as "WEAK FIT" on score alone. But it's fundamentally broken: it only passes in three dimensions.

The dual threshold prevents this. Both *breadth* and *depth* of quality are required.

## Fit Classification

| Fit | Total Score | Passes Required | Emoji |
|-----|------------|-----------------|-------|
| **STRONG** | ≥18 | ≥6 | 🟢 |
| **MODERATE** | ≥12 | ≥4 | 🟡 |
| **WEAK** | ≥8 | any | 🟠 |
| **NOT FIT** | <8 | — | 🔴 |

A **STRONG** fit company is a genuine compounder candidate. **MODERATE** is worth watching — may qualify with improving fundamentals. **WEAK** has some quality signals but too many gaps. **NOT FIT** doesn't meet the minimum bar.

## The 8 KPIs

### 1. FCF Margin

**Pass threshold: ≥10%**

| Score | FCF Margin |
|-------|-----------|
| 3 | ≥20% |
| 2 | ≥10% |
| 1 | ≥5% |
| 0 | <5% |

FCF margin = Free Cash Flow / Revenue. This is the clearest signal of pricing power and capital-light operations. A company converting 20%+ of revenue into free cash is generating genuine surplus — not accounting profits that get consumed by capex.

**What it tells you**: Whether the company's reported earnings translate into actual cash that can be deployed (dividends, buybacks, acquisitions, debt paydown) without starving the business.

**Common patterns**:
- Software: 25-40% (high score)
- Payments/processors: 15-25% (high score)
- Industrial compounders: 10-20% (moderate score)
- Retail/manufacturing: 3-8% (low score)

### 2. Gross Margin

**Pass threshold: ≥40%**

| Score | Gross Margin |
|-------|-------------|
| 3 | ≥70% |
| 2 | ≥50% |
| 1 | ≥30% |
| 0 | <30% |

Gross margin reveals the moat. Software companies at 80%+ have near-zero marginal cost of revenue. But the pass threshold is deliberately 40% (not 60%+) because **industrials with switching costs** can qualify — they're not software, but their customers are just as locked in.

**What it tells you**: Pricing power and cost structure. A company that can charge 3-5× its cost of goods sold has something customers can't easily replace with a cheaper alternative.

**Why 40%, not 60%**: A capital-intensive sector company at 40% GM may have a *stronger* moat than a SaaS company at 80% because the switching costs are physical, not just contractual. Jack Henry (JKHY) at ~48% GM is a perfect compounder with a regulatory moat that no 80% GM SaaS company can match.

### 3. Revenue Stability

**Pass threshold: ≤25% volatility**

| Score | Revenue Volatility |
|-------|-------------------|
| 3 | ≤10% |
| 2 | ≤20% |
| 1 | ≤30% |
| 0 | >30% |

Revenue volatility measures year-over-year consistency. Compounders should have *boring* revenue. If it swings wildly, the business is cyclical or discretionary — not a compounder.

**What it tells you**: Whether the business is structurally recurring or economically sensitive. A company with ≤10% volatility has utility-like predictability.

**What it catches**: Companies that look profitable at peak but are cyclical. A semiconductor company might have 30% FCF margins at peak and -5% at trough — the volatility flag catches this.

### 4. Growth Profile

**Pass threshold: −5% to +25%**

| Score | Revenue Growth (3Y CAGR) |
|-------|-------------------------|
| 3 | 2–15% |
| 2 | ≤2% |
| 1 | ≤25% |
| 0 | >25% or <−5% |

This is deliberately **not "higher is better"**. The sweet spot is 2–15% — steady, sustainable growth. The maximum score goes to companies growing at moderate, predictable rates.

**Why not reward high growth?**
- Above 25% often signals a very small base, unsustainable expansion, or cyclical peak
- Companies growing 8% consistently for 20 years are more attractive than those growing 30% for 3 years then stalling
- Slow growth (≤2%) gets a 2 — it's not exciting, but it's sustainable (think utilities, mature software)

**What it tells you**: Whether the company is in a sustainable growth phase or riding a temporary wave.

### 5. FCF Yield

**Pass threshold: ≥2%**

| Score | FCF Yield |
|-------|-----------|
| 3 | ≥5% |
| 2 | ≥3% |
| 1 | ≥2% |
| 0 | <2% |

FCF Yield = Free Cash Flow / Enterprise Value. This answers: *how much cash is this business generating relative to what you'd pay?*

**What it tells you**: Whether the stock is reasonably priced relative to its cash generation. A 5%+ FCF yield on a quality compounder means the market is undervaluing the cash generation. Below 2% means you're paying for future growth that may not materialize.

**Use in practice**: FCF yield is the bridge between quality and price. A STRONG FIT company at 1% FCF yield is still overpriced. A MODERATE FIT company at 6% FCF yield might be a bargain.

### 6. Debt Discipline

**Pass threshold: D/E ≤2.0**

| Score | Debt/Equity |
|-------|-------------|
| 3 | ≤0.5 |
| 2 | ≤1.0 |
| 1 | ≤2.0 |
| 0 | >2.0 |

Compounders shouldn't need leverage. If the business genuinely generates excess cash, why carry debt? Low debt also means the company can weather downturns without risking the business.

**What it tells you**: Capital allocation discipline and financial resilience. A score of 3 (D/E ≤0.5) signals a company that's truly self-funding.

**Why it matters for compounders**: Debt amplifies both returns and risks. A leveraged company might show high ROE, but that's financial engineering, not business quality. True compounders generate high returns on *unleveraged* capital.

### 7. Size Fit

**Pass threshold: ≥$200M market cap**

| Score | Market Cap |
|-------|-----------|
| 3 | $500M–$10B |
| 2 | $200–500M or $10–50B |
| 1 | Anything above $200M |
| 0 | <$200M |

The sweet spot is mid-cap ($500M–$10B) — big enough to be established and liquid, small enough to still compound.

**Why not large caps?** Above $50B, the law of large numbers makes compounding mathematically harder. A $50B company growing 15% requires $7.5B in *new* revenue each year.

**Why not micro caps?** Below $200M, liquidity is poor, information is scarce, and the business may not be durable enough to survive a downturn. This isn't a venture capital tool.

### 8. Moat Quality

**Pass: GM ≥40% OR capital-intensive sector**

| Score | Rule |
|-------|------|
| 3 | Gross Margin ≥60% |
| 2 | GM ≥40%, OR capital-intensive sector |
| 0 | GM <40% in non-capital-intensive sector |

This is a sector-aware heuristic, not a raw metric. The reasoning:

- Software with 70%+ GM clearly has a moat (near-zero marginal cost)
- An industrial company at 40% GM in a sector where 25% is the norm has a moat *relative to its peers* — they're earning above-sector margins because of switching costs or scale
- Capital-intensive sectors (industrials, energy, materials, real estate) get a pass because their moats are structural (physical assets, regulatory licenses, geographic monopolies), not margin-based

**Capital-intensive sectors that get the pass:**
- Industrials
- Energy
- Materials
- Real Estate

## Score Breakdown Display

In the UI, each KPI shows as ✅ (pass) or ❌ (fail) with the score:

```
📊 Score Breakdown
✅ FCF Margin: 2/3 (12.5%)
✅ Gross Margin: 3/3 (72.0%)
✅ Revenue Stability: 3/3 (8.2%)
✅ Growth Profile: 2/3 (1.5%)
❌ FCF Yield: 1/3 (1.8%)
✅ Debt Discipline: 3/3 (0.3)
✅ Size Fit: 3/3 ($2,100M)
✅ Moat Quality: 3/3 (software)

Total: 18/24 | Passes: 7/8 | FIT: 🟢 STRONG
```

When data is missing for a KPI (yfinance doesn't have it), the display shows ⚪ N/A instead of ❌ ?/3.
