# The CSU Compounder Framework

## Core Thesis

A **compounder** is a company with a durable competitive advantage that generates strong, growing free cash flow, requires little capital to grow, and is extremely hard for customers to replace.

The ideal compounder is a **mission-critical, low-cost part of the customer's operations** — so entrenched that switching is either too expensive, too risky, or both.

> The customer spends $1M/year with you, but replacing you would cost $5M and risk a year of disruption.

That's the moat. And it's not just about software — it applies to industrial processors, payment networks, data providers, and any business where the cost of switching exceeds the cost of staying.

## Why "Compounder" Not "Growth Stock"?

Growth stocks require you to be right about the *future*. Compounders require you to be right about the *present*.

A compounder's default outcome is that it keeps compounding. Revenue grows because customers don't leave, new ones arrive organically, and pricing power lets margins expand over time. You don't need the company to "win a market" or "disrupt an industry" — you just need it to keep doing what it's already doing.

This means:
- **Lower risk**: The business model is proven, not speculative
- **Time is your friend**: Each year of compounding makes the next year easier
- **Kill signals are observable**: You know what would break the thesis — and you can watch for it

## The Five Pillars

### 1. Low Customer Cost Concentration

The company's product/service represents a small fraction of the customer's total costs. This means:
- Customers don't aggressively negotiate price (it's not worth their time)
- The product isn't a target for cost-cutting during downturns
- Price increases stick because they're immaterial to the customer's P&L

**Example**: A bank spending $500K/year on core processing software out of $2B in operating expenses. The software is mission-critical, but the cost is invisible in the budget.

### 2. High Switching Costs

Leaving is more expensive than staying. Switching costs can be:
- **Financial**: Implementation fees, dual-running costs, migration expenses
- **Operational**: Downtime risk, retraining, workflow disruption
- **Regulatory**: Compliance recertification, audit requirements
- **Technical**: Data migration, integration rewriting, proprietary formats
- **Psychological**: Nobody gets fired for keeping the incumbent

**Example**: Replacing a hospital's EMR system costs $50-100M and takes 2-3 years. Even a mediocre EMR that's already installed is "sticky" by default.

### 3. Recurring Revenue

Revenue that renews automatically, not revenue that must be resold every year. The spectrum:

| Type | Renewal Effort | Predictability |
|------|----------------|----------------|
| True subscription | Zero (auto-renew) | Highest |
| Contract-based | Low (multi-year deals) | High |
| Consumptive | None (usage-based) | Medium-High |
| Transactional | High (resell each time) | Low |
| Project-based | Very high | Very low |

Compounders should be in the top 3 rows. If revenue must be resold every quarter, it's not a compounder — it's a consultancy.

### 4. Mission-Critical Products

The customer's operations would materially suffer if the product disappeared. This creates:
- **Inelastic demand**: The product isn't discretionary
- **Pricing power**: The vendor can raise prices without losing customers
- **Retention**: Churn is structurally low, not just contractually enforced

**Test**: If the product went down for a day, would the customer's operations stop? If yes, it's mission-critical.

### 5. Capital-Light Growth

The business can grow without proportionally increasing capital expenditure. This means:
- Incremental revenue flows largely to FCF, not back into capex
- Returns on incremental capital are high (20%+)
- The company doesn't need to raise equity or take on debt to grow
- Buybacks and dividends are funded organically, not with leverage

## What the Framework Rejects

Just as important as what it looks for:

| Rejection | Why |
|-----------|-----|
| **Cyclicals** with volatile revenue | Profitable at peak ≠ compounder |
| **Story stocks** with high growth but no FCF | Growth without cash is burning money |
| **Leveraged compounders** | Debt-fueled buybacks aren't real compounding |
| **Low-margin industrials** in competitive sectors | No moat signal — margins will compress |
| **Micro-caps** below $200M | Liquidity and durability risk |
| **Companies growing >25%/yr** | Likely unsustainable or cyclical peak |

## Broader Than Software

A key design decision: the framework is **not limited to software**. While SaaS companies naturally score well (high margins, recurring revenue, switching costs), the scoring thresholds are deliberately set to capture compounders in any sector.

An industrial company with:
- 45% gross margin (above sector average)
- 12% FCF margin
- 5% steady growth
- D/E of 0.3
- 15% revenue volatility

...scores as **MODERATE FIT** and may be a better compounder than a SaaS company at 80% GM growing 30%/yr (which scores poorly on growth profile and revenue stability).

The moat quality KPI explicitly accounts for this: capital-intensive sectors (industrials, energy, materials) get a pass on gross margin because their moats are structural, not margin-based.

## The Time Horizon

This is a **long-term framework**. The expected holding period is years, not months. The scoring system, valuation method, and kill signals are all designed for investors who want to own businesses, not trade stocks.

If you're looking for quick flips, this is the wrong tool. If you're looking for businesses that will be worth more in 5-10 years than they are today — and you want to know when that thesis breaks — you're in the right place.
