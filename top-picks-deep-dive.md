## 1. Jack Henry & Associates (JKHY) — 🥇 THE CSU Archetype

### Business Model
Core banking technology for community banks and credit unions (7,500+ institutions). Three segments: Core processing (the "brain" of the bank — general ledger, deposits, loans), Payments (card processing, ACH, bill pay, real-time payments), and Complementary (digital banking via Banno, fraud/risk, lending). 91% recurring revenue. Outsourcing contracts typically 6+ years with built-in price escalators. Revenue model: fees based on number of accounts/users/assets — grows alongside customers organically.

### Switching Costs: 🟢 Extreme (Technical + Regulatory + Data)
Core banking is the operating system of a bank. Switching = migrating all customer data, retraining all staff, reconfiguring all integrations, regulatory re-validation. A core conversion takes 12-24 months and costs millions. It's the financial equivalent of open-heart surgery — banks only do it when forced to (acquisition by a megabank using a different system). JKHY receives "deconversion fees" (~$25M/year) when this happens — they literally get paid when customers leave.

### % of Customer's Costs: 🟢 Classic CSU (~0.1-0.5%)
For a community bank with $1-5B in assets, core processing might cost $500K-$2M/year. That's a tiny fraction of total operating costs (salaries, branches, compliance). No one is cutting this to save money.

### Mission Criticality: 🟢 Maximum
If core banking goes down, the bank cannot process transactions, cannot operate. It's the definition of mission-critical infrastructure. Payroll processing + regulatory compliance = regulatory risk if it fails.

### Retention: 🟢 99%+
"Jack Henry retains more than 99% of its revenue each year before involuntary consolidation." Only lose customers when a megabank acquires them and converts to a different core. Even then, they get deconversion fees.

### Competitive Landscape
The "Big Three" core providers: Fiserv (FI, $78B market cap), FIS ($40B+), and JKHY ($11B). JKHY is the smallest but dominates community banks and credit unions. NPS scores consistently higher than Fiserv/FIS. Symitar is the #1 core system for credit unions with >$1B in assets. Born-in-the-cloud competitors (Thought Machine, Mambu) exist but target larger/enterprise banks, not JKHY's niche.

### AI Risk: 🟢 Low — AI is an enabler
JKHY integrated AI-driven predictive tools into fraud detection and credit risk in late 2025. AI enhances their product. Core banking is not something AI replaces — it's regulated infrastructure. The threat is AI enabling a cloud-native competitor, but JKHY is already migrating to cloud-native (76% on private cloud, moving to Google Cloud). The real risk is AI making it easier for competitors to build core systems — but regulatory barriers remain.

### Management
Greg Adelson (CEO since mid-2024, previously COO). David Foss (executive chairman, former CEO). Smooth transition. Conservative culture — avoids annual restructurings and transformational acquisitions. CTO Ben Metz driving cloud-native "Jack Henry Platform" initiative. $150M in debt (0.2x leverage). Not aggressive enough on capital allocation per some analysts — but that's a feature, not a bug, for CSU-style investors.

### Valuation: 🟡 Fair
- P/E: ~27x (below 3/5/10yr averages but above 2013-2018 averages)
- P/FCF: ~27x
- FCF Yield: 3.6%
- Fair value estimate: ~$170 (Bob Hammel / Substack analyst)
- Current price ~$156 → slightly below fair value

### Thesis
**Bull:** The closest public-market equivalent to a CSU acquisition — 99%+ retention, regulatory switching costs, 0.1% of customer costs, mission-critical, growing 7% organically with built-in price escalators. The cloud transition is a multi-year tailwind. Unrivaled in community banks/credit unions. AI enhances, not disrupts. Fortress balance sheet with optionality for buybacks or acquisitions.

**Bear:** Bank consolidation slowly shrinks the customer base (community banks acquired by megabanks). Multiple compression from 37x to 27x despite 9.4% EPS CAGR — market may not re-rate. FCF conversion dropped from ~90% to ~69% due to tax code changes. Conservative capital allocation means cash piles up unproductively.

**Kill signal:** Retention drops below 98% OR a cloud-native core provider gains real traction in the community bank segment.

## 2. Veeva Systems (VEEV) — Life Sciences Infrastructure

### Business Model
Industry cloud for life sciences. Two pillars: Veeva CRM (pharma sales force management, built on Salesforce architecture but now transitioning to own platform) and Veeva Vault (regulatory document management, clinical trial management, quality/compliance). Revenue ~$2.75B in FY2025, growing mid-teens, with subscription revenue growing ~20%. Serves 1,000+ life sciences customers including all top 20 pharma companies.

### Switching Costs: 🟢 Very High (Regulatory + Data + Process)
Once a pharma company standardizes on Veeva Vault for clinical trial documentation and regulatory submissions, switching means re-validating entire workflows with the FDA/EMA. Validated processes, complex data structures, and extensive user training create massive lock-in. Contract tenures are long. "Mission-critical: it underpins trial documentation, regulatory submissions, safety reporting, and promotional compliance."

### % of Customer's Costs: 🟢 <1%
For a pharma company spending $5B+ on R&D annually, Veeva costs are a rounding error. Clinical trial management software is not where you cut costs.

### Mission Criticality: 🟢 Maximum
Regulatory submissions cannot be delayed. Clinical trial data must be audit-ready. Safety reporting is legally mandated. If Vault goes down, trials could be non-compliant.

### Retention: 🟡 ~95%
Not as extreme as JKHY's 99%+. Some risk of Salesforce Health competing. But the Vault ecosystem is deeply embedded.

### Competitive Landscape
Veeva dominates life sciences CRM (built the vertical Salesforce never could). Vault competitors include Medidata (Dassault), Oracle Life Sciences, but Veeva has the industry standard position. The transition from Salesforce platform to own "Vault CRM" is a risk (execution risk) but also an opportunity (no more Salesforce dependency).

### AI Risk: 🟡 Medium — Mixed
AI enhances drug discovery and clinical trial design (Veeva benefits from more trials = more Vault usage). But AI could also simplify regulatory compliance workflows, reducing the complexity that creates switching costs. Also, AI could enable smaller biotechs to manage regulatory processes without Veeva. Net: AI likely grows the pie (more drug candidates = more trials) but may erode pricing power long-term.

### Management
Peter Gassner (CEO, founder). Significant stock ownership. Visionary who saw the life sciences vertical opportunity at Salesforce and built Veeva into a $27B company. Transitioned from public benefit corporation back to standard C-corp in 2024 — practical move, not governance red flag.

### Valuation: 🔴 Expensive
- P/E: ~50x+
- P/FCF: ~25x
- FCF Yield: 5.3% (but inflated by high FCF margin — the growth is priced in)
- Market cap: $27B for $2.75B revenue = ~10x sales

### Thesis
**Bull:** The life sciences equivalent of JKHY — regulatory moat, mission-critical, low % of customer costs, high switching costs. Vault is becoming the operating system of pharma. AI grows the pie. Founder-led with skin in the game. The CRM-to-own-platform transition removes the Salesforce dependency.

**Bear:** At 50x earnings, you're paying for perfection. The Vault CRM transition is execution risk. Salesforce Health could compete more aggressively. AI may reduce the regulatory complexity that drives switching costs. Gross margins compressed during SaaS transition (79% from 85%).

**Kill signal:** Vault CRM migration causes customer churn OR Salesforce Health wins a major pharma account.

## 3. SS&C Technologies (SSNC) — Financial Plumbing

### Business Model
Software and services for financial services and healthcare. Six segments: Wealth Management (Addepar, Eze), Alternatives (fund administration, accounting), Insurance and Pension, Financial Markets (trading, risk), Banking, and Healthcare. $6.3B revenue in FY2025. Founded 1986 by William Stone (still CEO). Serial acquirer — bought ~60+ companies. 97% retention rate. Recurring revenue growth: 6.7%.

### Switching Costs: 🟢 High (Technical + Regulatory + Data)
Fund accounting, transfer agency, portfolio management — these are regulatory functions. Once SS&C processes your fund's NAV calculations and regulatory filings, switching requires parallel running and re-validation with regulators. Data migration from decades of historical records is a nightmare. 97% retention speaks for itself.

### % of Customer's Costs: 🟢 <1%
SS&C's fund administration fees are a tiny fraction of AUM-based management fees. No hedge fund cuts its administrator to save money — the risk of getting NAV wrong is existential.

### Mission Criticality: 🟢 Very High
NAV calculations, regulatory filings, trade processing — if these are wrong, the fund is in breach. Not optional.

### Retention: 🟢 97%
"Maintained a strong 97% retention rate" per FY2025 data. High value-added nature of products drives this.

### Competitive Landscape
Competes with State Street, BNY Mellon (in fund administration), SimCorp (in portfolio management), Bloomberg (in trading data). But SS&C has built scale through acquisition that's hard to replicate. Many competitors are banks (State Street, BNY) where software is a cost center, not a profit center.

### AI Risk: 🟢 Low
Financial regulatory infrastructure is the last thing AI disrupts. AI can help process documents faster (SS&C benefits), but replacing regulated fund accounting and transfer agency with AI is not happening. The liability alone makes it impossible.

### Management
William Stone (founder, CEO since 1986). Significant stock ownership (~8-10%). Aggressive acquirer (CSU-like in that regard). Somewhat controversial governance (dual-class shares). But the track record is clear — 35+ years of compounding.

### Valuation: 🟢 Cheap
- P/E: ~16x
- P/FCF: ~12x
- FCF Yield: 8.5%
- Market cap: $17B for $6.3B revenue

### Thesis
**Bull:** The closest thing to CSU in the US public markets — serial acquirer, 97% retention, regulated switching costs, low % of customer costs, 8.5% FCF yield. William Stone has been doing this for 35+ years. Financial infrastructure is AI-resistant. Extremely cheap on FCF yield.

**Bear:** Acquisition-driven growth can mask organic weakness. D/E of 1.11 means leverage is being used. Dual-class shares = governance risk. Some acquired businesses (Eze, Addepar) face competitive pressure. Revenue growth is modest (~6%).

**Kill signal:** Retention drops below 95% OR William Stone steps down without a clear succession plan.

## 4. Manhattan Associates (MANH) — Supply Chain Nerve Center

### Business Model
Supply chain commerce software. Three products: Manhattan Active Warehouse Management (WMS), Manhattan Active Omni (unified commerce/OMS), and Manhattan Active Inventory Management. 17-time Gartner Magic Quadrant leader in WMS. Cloud-native, microservices architecture. Revenue ~$1B, growing ~12% organically. FCF margin: 34% (exceptional).

### Switching Costs: 🟢 High (Technical + Process + Data)
A WMS is deeply embedded in warehouse operations — it controls every movement, pick, pack, and ship. Replacing it means retraining all warehouse workers, reconfiguring automation/robotics integrations, and potentially disrupting operations during migration. 17-time Gartner leader = deep institutional knowledge and best practices accumulated over decades.

### % of Customer's Costs: 🟡 ~1-2%
Higher than CSU's typical 0.1-1%. For a large retailer or 3PL, WMS costs might be $1-5M/year. Still small relative to total warehouse labor and operations costs ($50-200M+), but not as negligible as core banking software.

### Mission Criticality: 🟢 Very High
If the WMS goes down, the warehouse stops. Orders don't ship. Revenue is lost. It's the operating system of the warehouse.

### Retention: 🟡 ~95% (estimated)
Not publicly disclosed at the level of JKHY or SSNC. But WMS switching is painful enough that retention is high. The risk is competitors (Blue Yonder, SAP, Oracle) in large enterprise.

### Competitive Landscape
WMS market: Manhattan vs. Blue Yonder (JDA, now Panasonic), SAP EWM, Oracle, Körber (Infor). Manhattan leads in Gartner MQ. For retail/3PL, Manhattan is often the default choice. But enterprise ERP vendors (SAP, Oracle) try to bundle WMS — this is the main competitive threat.

### AI Risk: 🟡 Medium
AI could optimize warehouse operations (Manhattan is adding AI features), but could also enable simpler WMS alternatives. The risk is that AI makes it easier to configure and run a warehouse without the deep process knowledge that Manhattan embeds. Near-term: AI is an enabler. Long-term: AI could commoditize some WMS functions.

### Management
Eddie Capel (CEO since 2013). Long tenure, steady execution. Not a flashy founder — more of an operator. This is a feature for CSU-style investing.

### Valuation: 🔴 Expensive
- P/E: ~55x
- P/FCF: ~28x
- FCF Yield: 4.4%
- Market cap: $8.5B for ~$1B revenue = ~8.5x sales

### Thesis
**Bull:** Best-in-class WMS with extreme switching costs and mission-critical positioning. 34% FCF margin is elite. Cloud-native transition driving recurring revenue. AI features adding value. Dominant in retail/3PL vertical.

**Bear:** At 55x earnings, the market is pricing in perfection. Revenue concentration risk — large retail/3PL customers. Competition from SAP/Oracle bundles. AI could simplify WMS over time, reducing the process-knowledge moat. Higher % of customer costs than ideal CSU fit.

**Kill signal:** A major retailer switches to a competitor OR AI-native WMS startup wins significant enterprise accounts.

## 5. FactSet (FDS) — Financial Data Infrastructure

### Business Model
Financial data and analytics platform. Provides data feeds, analytics, research tools, and workflow solutions to investment professionals (asset managers, hedge funds, investment banks). ~$2.2B revenue. 91% client retention. Subscription-based model with extensive API library and integrated workflows.

### Switching Costs: 🟢 High (Data + Process + Integration)
Analysts build years of custom models, screens, and workflows on FactSet. The API library integrates with trading systems, risk platforms, and internal tools. Once a firm standardizes on FactSet, the accumulated intellectual property (models, screens, templates) creates enormous lock-in. "When clients start using FactSet's extensive data API library and integrated workflow, they rarely leave."

### % of Customer's Costs: 🟡 ~1-3%
Higher than ideal. For a hedge fund, data/analytics might be $500K-$2M/year — not trivial relative to total operating costs. This makes FactSet more vulnerable to cost-cutting pressure than a 0.1% cost item.

### Mission Criticality: 🟡 Moderate-High
Important for investment decisions, but not "the bank stops operating" critical. A fund can switch data providers (painful but survivable). Bloomberg terminal is the real competitor here.

### Retention: 🟡 91%
Good but not exceptional. Some client churn is normal in this space. FactSet loses some clients to Bloomberg, Capital IQ, or in-house data teams.

### Competitive Landscape
Bloomberg Terminal (dominant in real-time trading data), Capital IQ (S&P Global), Refinitiv (LSEG), Morningstar Direct. FactSet's niche: deep analytics and customizable data for portfolio managers and analysts. Not as dominant as Bloomberg in trading, but stronger in research/analytics workflows.

### AI Risk: 🟡 Medium-High
AI could fundamentally change how analysts consume financial data. If AI can synthesize data from multiple sources, the value of a single integrated platform diminishes. FactSet is investing in AI (Document Search, AI workflows) to defend, but the risk is real. This is a space where AI disruption is plausible.

### Management
Phil Snow (CEO). Steady but not transformative. FactSet has historically been a "good but not great" compounder — solid returns, but not the explosive growth of a Veeva or Manhattan.

### Valuation: 🟡 Fair
- P/E: ~28x
- P/FCF: ~18x
- FCF Yield: 6.9% (high due to strong FCF margin of 25.7%)
- Market cap: $9B

### Thesis
**Bull:** Strong data moat with high switching costs from accumulated models and integrations. 6.9% FCF yield is attractive. AI investments (Document Search, workflow automation) could deepen engagement and drive retention higher.

**Bear:** AI is the biggest threat — if analysts can get synthesized insights without a single platform, the switching cost erodes. 91% retention is good but not JKHY-level. Higher % of customer costs makes it a target for procurement cuts. Bloomberg is the 800-lb gorilla.

**Kill signal:** Retention drops below 88% OR an AI-native financial data platform wins significant market share.

## 6. PTC Inc (PTC) — Industrial Software Platform

### Business Model
Industrial software: CAD (Creo — mechanical design), PLM (Windchill — product lifecycle management), IoT/AR (ThingWorx, Vuforia), and Service Lifecycle Management. Transitioning from perpetual licenses to subscription. Revenue ~$2.2B. FCF margin: 30% (elite). FCF yield: 5.1%.

### Switching Costs: 🟢 High (Technical + Process + File Format)
CAD files in proprietary formats (Creo) create format lock-in. PLM databases accumulate years of product data, BOMs, and change records. Re-training thousands of engineers on a new CAD tool is enormously expensive and disruptive. Windchill manages the entire product lifecycle — replacing it means reconfiguring workflows across multiple departments.

### % of Customer's Costs: 🟢 <1%
PTC software costs are tiny relative to the engineers using it and the products being designed. No one cuts CAD licenses to save money when engineer salaries are $100K+ each.

### Mission Criticality: 🟢 High
Engineers can't design without CAD. Manufacturing can't access BOMs without PLM. Product development stops without these tools.

### Retention: 🟡 ~90% (estimated)
CAD/PLM switching is painful but happens — usually during major platform refreshes (every 10-15 years). PTC faces pressure from Siemens NX/Teamcenter and Dassault SolidWorks/CATIA.

### Competitive Landscape
The CAD/PLM "Big Three": PTC vs. Siemens (NX, Teamcenter, Solid Edge) vs. Dassault Systèmes (CATIA, SolidWorks, ENOVIA). Autodesk (Fusion 360) is the mid-market disruptor. PTC is #2-3 in CAD but strong in IoT/AR. The IoT story is the differentiator but also the risk — less proven than core CAD/PLM.

### AI Risk: 🟡 Medium
AI-assisted design (generative design) is being integrated into all CAD platforms — PTC is adding AI features to Creo. Near-term, AI is an enabler (better tools = more value). Long-term, if AI makes CAD simpler, the process-knowledge moat could erode. But proprietary file formats remain a strong lock-in mechanism.

### Management
Jim Heppelmann (CEO, long tenure). Steady leader who has navigated the subscription transition and IoT acquisitions. Not a founder, but deeply knowledgeable about the industry.

### Valuation: 🟢 Fair-Attractive
- P/E: ~30x
- P/FCF: ~20x
- FCF Yield: 5.1%
- Market cap: $16.7B for ~$2.2B revenue

### Thesis
**Bull:** Deep industrial moat via proprietary file formats and process lock-in. 30% FCF margin and 5.1% FCF yield is exceptional for a software company. AI enhances CAD productivity. IoT/AR is optionality. Low % of customer costs = CSU-like defensibility.

**Bear:** Revenue growth is modest (~5-7%). IoT strategy has been expensive with mixed results. Competition from Siemens and Dassault is fierce — they have larger installed bases. Subscription transition still in progress — some revenue volatility.

**Kill signal:** A major customer (e.g., automotive OEM) switches from Creo to a competitor OR IoT revenue stalls for multiple quarters.

## 7. Keysight Technologies (KEYS) — Electronic Test & Measurement

### Business Model
Electronic test and measurement equipment and software. Serves aerospace/defense, communications (5G), semiconductor, automotive/EV, and general electronics. Products range from oscilloscopes to network analyzers to simulation software (PathWave). Revenue ~$5.2B (cyclical — was $6.2B in FY2024, declining due to semiconductor cycle). 36% recurring revenue (software/services), growing toward 40%.

### Switching Costs: 🟢 High (Technical + Calibration + Process)
Customers invest heavily in training and process development around Keysight's hardware and integrated software (PathWave). Moving to a competitor requires costly overhaul of design and testing workflows. Calibration standards create additional lock-in — once you're calibrated to Keysight's standards, switching introduces measurement risk.

### % of Customer's Costs: 🟢 <1%
Test equipment is a tiny fraction of product development costs. No one at Apple says "let's save money on oscilloscopes."

### Mission Criticality: 🟢 High
If your 5G chip doesn't pass Keysight's compliance test, it doesn't ship. Test results are required for regulatory certification. This is non-negotiable.

### Retention: 🟡 ~90% (estimated, hardware + software mix)
Hardware has less lock-in than software, but the PathWave software ecosystem is increasingly sticky. Recurring revenue (28% ARR) is growing — this is the future moat.

### Competitive Landscape
Rohde & Schwarz (private, German — the main competitor in high-end), Tektronix (Fortive), Anritsu (Japanese), National Instruments (now Emerson). In 5G/6G, Keysight is the leader. In general-purpose oscilloscopes, Tektronix competes. No big tech company is entering this space — too niche.

### AI Risk: 🟢 Low
AI needs more chips → chips need more testing → Keysight benefits. AI data centers drive demand for high-speed interconnect testing. AI doesn't replace a $500K network analyzer. The physics of measurement doesn't change because of software.

### Management
Satish Dhanasekaran (CEO since 2023, previously COO). Taking over from Ron Nersesian who built the company through acquisition (Agilent spinoff → Keysight). $3.12B cash on balance sheet. $150M buyback in Q2 2025.

### Valuation: 🟢 Attractive
- P/E: ~25x (but earnings are cyclically depressed)
- P/FCF: ~22x
- FCF Yield: 2.2% (but FCF depressed by cycle)
- Market cap: $58B — this is now a large cap
- Cyclical trough = potential buying opportunity

### Thesis
**Bull:** The TSMC of test equipment — accumulated technical knowledge in RF/microwave that's irreplicable. AI/data center buildout drives secular demand. Physics-based moat (calibration, measurement standards) that AI cannot disrupt. 28% and growing ARR provides downside protection in cycles. $3.1B cash = optionality.

**Bear:** Highly cyclical (semiconductor/A&D cycles). Revenue declining from $6.2B to ~$5.2B. At $58B market cap, you're buying a cyclical at a growth-stock multiple. Gross margin (62%) is lower than pure software — this is hardware with software attached, not the other way around. Not a true CSU fit — it's a different model.

**Kill signal:** Semi cycle doesn't recover by mid-2026 OR recurring revenue growth stalls.

## 8. Paychex (PAYX) — SMB Payroll Infrastructure

### Business Model
Payroll processing, HR services, and benefits administration for small and mid-sized businesses (SMBs). ~700K clients. Revenue: $5.6B (FY2025), growing ~6-7%. FCF margin: 43% (extraordinary). FCF yield: 5.1%. Also provides 401(k) recordkeeping, time & attendance, PEO (Professional Employer Organization) services. Revenue model: per-employee per-pay-period — grows as clients hire more people.

### Switching Costs: 🟡 Moderate-High (Regulatory + Process)
Payroll switching is painful — tax filings, compliance, direct deposits, benefits enrollment all need to transition. But it's not as complex as core banking. The per-employee pricing model is a retention driver (scales naturally). 6-year+ typical client relationship.

### % of Customer's Costs: 🟡 ~1-3%
For a 50-person small business, Paychex might cost $15K-$25K/year. That's meaningful relative to a small business's total costs — more visible than JKHY's core banking fees. This makes PAYX somewhat more vulnerable to cost-cutting than ideal.

### Mission Criticality: 🟢 High
If payroll doesn't run, employees don't get paid, taxes aren't filed, and the business faces regulatory penalties. This is non-negotiable.

### Retention: 🟡 ~90%
Good but not exceptional. SMBs are more likely to switch than large enterprises. Gusto and QuickBooks Payroll are winning new SMBs at the low end. ADP is the main competitor at scale.

### Competitive Landscape
ADP (much larger, $90B+ market cap), Gusto (private, modern UI), Intuit/QuickBooks Payroll, and various PEOs. Paychex dominates the mid-market SMB (20-500 employees). ADP dominates enterprise. Gusto wins the sub-20 employee segment.

### AI Risk: 🟡 Medium
AI could simplify payroll/HR compliance, making it easier for SMBs to self-serve. But regulatory complexity (state-by-state tax rules, benefits compliance) creates a structural moat that AI alone can't overcome. Paychex is integrating AI into its platform (fraud detection, compliance automation). Near-term: enabler. Long-term: AI-native payroll could commoditize the middle market.

### Management
Tom Golisano (founder) is no longer involved in operations. Current CEO: John Gibson (since 2021). Steady operator. The company is known for conservative management and consistent execution. No dramatic moves — good for compounding, bad for excitement.

### Valuation: 🟡 Fair
- P/E: ~27x
- P/FCF: ~22x
- FCF Yield: 5.1%
- Market cap: $33B

### Thesis
**Bull:** 43% FCF margin is elite. Payroll is mission-critical infrastructure with regulatory switching costs. Per-employee pricing = natural revenue growth as clients hire. 5.1% FCF yield with 6-7% growth = double-digit total return. Defensive in recessions (payroll doesn't stop).

**Bear:** SMB market is competitive — Gusto and QuickBooks are winning the low end. 90% retention is good but not CSU-level. Higher % of customer costs than ideal = procurement target. Limited growth runway in domestic SMB (already penetrated). AI could simplify compliance, reducing the moat.

**Kill signal:** Retention drops below 87% OR a major SMB platform (like Stripe or Square) bundles payroll successfully.

## 9. Varonis Systems (VRNS) — Data Security Infrastructure

### Business Model
Data security and governance platform. Maps, analyzes, manages, and migrates unstructured data (files, emails, SharePoint, cloud). Core products: DatAdvantage (data access governance), DataPrivilege (permissions management), IDU Classification Framework (data classification), DatAnywhere (file synchronization). Revenue: $624M (FY2025, up from $551M in FY2024). Mid-transition from on-premise to SaaS. FCF: ~$80M in FY2025, guidance >$100M in FY2026.

### Switching Costs: 🟡 Moderate (Data + Process)
Varonis embeds deeply into an organization's data infrastructure — it maps who has access to what across millions of files. Once deployed, the accumulated access policies and data classifications create stickiness. But the switching cost is more "process" than "regulatory" — it's painful but not impossible. Less lock-in than JKHY or VEEV.

### % of Customer's Costs: 🟢 <1%
Data security software is a tiny fraction of IT budgets. Typically $100-500K for a mid-size enterprise vs. $50-200M+ total IT spend.

### Mission Criticality: 🟡 Moderate
Important for security posture, but not "the business stops operating" critical. A company can function without Varonis — they just have worse data governance. This is fundamentally different from JKHY (banks can't operate) or VEEV (trials stop).

### Retention: 🟡 ~90% (estimated, improving with SaaS)
The SaaS transition is improving retention (from ~85% perpetual to ~95% SaaS-like). But not at JKHY/SSNC levels yet.

### Competitive Landscape
Competes with broad cybersecurity vendors (Microsoft Purview, CrowdStrike, Palo Alto) and niche data governance players (SailPoint, CyberArk for identity). Varonis's niche (unstructured data governance) is narrow and deep. The risk is Microsoft building this into M365 natively (they're trying).

### AI Risk: 🔴 High
AI is a double-edged sword. Varonis uses AI for data classification and threat detection (enabler). But AI also makes it easier to build data governance tools — the specialized classification engine that took Varonis years to build could be replicated faster with LLMs. Microsoft's AI investments could make their built-in Purview good enough. This is the most AI-threatened company on the list.

### Management
Yaki Faitelson (co-founder, former CEO) stepped down in 2022. Current CEO: Brian T. Murphy. Transition period. Founder departure is a risk signal — CSU's lesson: founder-led companies with deep domain knowledge are harder to replicate.

### Valuation: 🔴 Expensive
- P/E: N/M (barely profitable on GAAP basis due to SaaS transition)
- P/FCF: ~35x+
- FCF Yield: 4.5% (but FCF just turned meaningful — $80M on $2.95B market cap)
- Market cap: $2.95B for $624M revenue
